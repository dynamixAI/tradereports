## The short answer

- **Business customers (landlords, letting agents, builders, shops, companies):** yes. The [Late Payment of Commercial Debts (Interest) Act 1998](https://www.gov.uk/late-commercial-payments-interest-debt-recovery) gives you the right automatically, even if your invoice never mentioned it.
- **Homeowners:** the Act doesn't cover sales to the public. You can only charge interest if it was in the terms the customer agreed to before the work started, and the terms have to be fair.

So if a lot of your work is for businesses, it's worth knowing exactly what you can claim.

## What you can claim from a business

1. **Interest** at 8% a year above the Bank of England base rate.
2. **A fixed fee for each late invoice**, to cover the cost of chasing:

| Size of the unpaid invoice | Fixed fee you can add |
| -------------------------- | --------------------- |
| Up to £999.99              | £40                   |
| £1,000 to £9,999.99        | £70                   |
| £10,000 or more            | £100                  |

3. **Reasonable extra costs** of recovering the debt (such as a debt collector) if they come to more than the fixed fee.

## Which rate to use

The base rate used is fixed twice a year, not the rate on the day you claim:

- An invoice that goes late between **1 January and 30 June** uses the base rate on the previous **31 December**.
- An invoice that goes late between **1 July and 31 December** uses the base rate on **30 June**.

| Invoice went late          | Base rate used | Interest rate you can charge |
| -------------------------- | -------------- | ---------------------------- |
| 1 January to 30 June 2026  | 3.75%          | 11.75% a year                |
| 1 July to 31 December 2026 | 3.75%          | 11.75% a year                |

The Bank of England raised the base rate to 4% on 5 November 2026. If it's still 4% on 31 December, invoices that go late in the first half of 2027 will carry 12%.

## When does an invoice count as late?

- If you agreed a payment date, it's late the day after that date.
- If you didn't agree one, payment is due **30 days** after the customer received your invoice or after the work was done, whichever is later.

## How to work it out

> Interest = invoice amount × interest rate ÷ 365 × days late

For example, a business owes you £2,400 and is 30 days late, at 11.75%:

- Interest per day: £2,400 × 11.75% ÷ 365 = **£0.77**
- 30 days of interest: **£23.18**
- Fixed fee (invoice between £1,000 and £9,999.99): **£70**
- **Total you can add: £93.18**

The interest keeps adding up every day until they pay.

## How to claim it

Tell the customer in writing, with the figures:

> Invoice [number] for £[amount] was due on [date] and is now [number] days overdue. Under the Late Payment of Commercial Debts (Interest) Act 1998 I'm adding statutory interest at [rate]% a year (£[daily amount] a day, £[total] so far) and a fixed late payment fee of £[40/70/100]. The total now due is £[total]. Please pay by [date].

Many tradespeople never actually collect it. Mentioning it is often enough to move your invoice to the top of the pile.

## Should you charge it?

It's your right, but it's also a relationship. With a good customer who is late once, a reminder may be better than a bill for interest. With a customer who is always late, charging it, or at least saying you will, usually changes their habits. Either way, putting "Late payments may be charged interest under the Late Payment of Commercial Debts (Interest) Act 1998" on your invoices costs nothing.

## What's changing

The government's late payment bill (introduced to Parliament in May 2026) would cap payment terms at 60 days when large businesses pay smaller suppliers, make late payment interest compulsory in business contracts, and give the [Small Business Commissioner](https://www.smallbusinesscommissioner.gov.uk/) powers to fine persistent late payers. As of November 2026 it's still going through Parliament, so it isn't law yet. The current rules above still apply.
