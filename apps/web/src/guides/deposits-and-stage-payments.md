On a big job you can be thousands of pounds out of pocket in materials and wages before you send a single invoice. Asking for money up front, and in stages as the work goes on, protects your cash flow and makes it much less likely you'll end up chasing a large final bill.

## Is it OK to ask for a deposit?

Yes. There's no law against it. What matters is that it's **agreed in writing before you start**, usually on your quote. Most customers expect it on larger jobs, especially when you're buying materials for them.

## How to set it out

Put the payment stages on the quote, tied to points in the job that the customer can see:

| Stage         | When                                           | Example on a £12,000 kitchen fit |
| ------------- | ---------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------- |
| Deposit       | When the quote is accepted, to order materials | £3,000                           |
| Stage payment | After the units are fitted                     | £5,000                           |
| Final payment | On completion                                  | £4,000                           |

The figures are just an example. Base yours on what you have to spend up front, especially materials ordered specially for that customer. Small, quick jobs usually don't need stages at all.

## How to ask

Keep it matter-of-fact. It's how you work, not a sign you don't trust them:

> To book your job in, I take a deposit of £[amount], which covers ordering your materials. Then £[amount] once [stage] is done, and the balance of £[amount] when the job's finished. I'll send an invoice for each one.

Customers rarely push back on a deposit that's clearly explained. If they do, that tells you something about how the final payment might go.

## The 14-day cancellation rule for homeowners

This catches a lot of tradespeople out. If you agree a job with a **homeowner** **in their home** (or over the phone or online), the law gives them **14 days to cancel** without giving a reason, under the Consumer Contracts Regulations.

- **You must tell them about this right in writing** (paper or email both count) and give them a cancellation form, before the contract is agreed.
- **If you start within the 14 days**, the customer has to ask you to in writing, for example by email or text. If they then cancel, they pay for the work done up to that point.
- **If they cancel in time, you refund the deposit**, less any work already done if they'd asked you to start early.
- **Leaving the cancellation information out is serious.** The 14 days can stretch to up to 12 months, and Trading Standards can take action.

This doesn't apply to jobs agreed at your own premises, or to business customers.

## Protect yourself

- **A written quote** that sets out the work, the price, the payment stages and your payment terms.
- **An invoice and a receipt for every payment**, deposit included. It keeps your records straight and avoids "I already paid that" arguments.
- **Don't let the stages slip.** If a stage payment is late, it's fine to pause before the next stage until it's paid, as long as your terms said so.
